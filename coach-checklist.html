The 7-question coach checklist.

Before you pay a youth football trainer for a single session, ask these. The good ones will answer in 30 seconds. The bad ones will dodge.

The seven questions

Question 1

"How many athletes have you trained in my kid's position?"

Why it matters: a generalist isn't useless, but a coach who's worked with 20 quarterbacks knows the QB-specific failure modes. A good answer is a number, not "a lot."

Question 2

"Can you give me two prior families I can call?"

Why it matters: if they hesitate, walk away. Coaches who've helped real families are proud to connect you. This is the single best filter you have.

Question 3

"What does a typical session look like?"

Why it matters: a real coach can describe a 60-minute structure (warmup, position work, situational drills, cool-down). If they wave it off as "we'll figure it out," they don't have one.

Question 4

"How do you measure if my kid is actually getting better?"

Why it matters: the good ones track something — 40-time, route accuracy, throw velocity, missed assignments per session. The bad ones just say "you'll see it on the field."

Question 5

"Can I watch the first session?"

Why it matters: real coaches welcome parent presence on day one — it builds trust and helps you understand what your kid is actually being taught. A coach who insists parents stay away from session one is hiding something.

Question 6

"Can my kid train solo, or only in groups?"

Why it matters: both formats can work, but you should know which one you're paying for. Group rates should be lower per kid; 1:1 should be priced accordingly.

Question 7

"How do we know it's working after three sessions?"

Why it matters: forces them to commit to a short-term checkpoint. If they say "you have to give it months," that's a yellow flag — every real coach can point to something measurable inside 3 sessions.

Red flags & green flags

Red flags

  • No references they'll share
  • Cash-only, no written agreement
  • Claims they "developed" famous players without specifics
  • Won't let parents watch the first session
  • Pressures you to commit to 10+ sessions upfront

Green flags

  • Shares prior family contacts proactively
  • Has liability insurance and will show proof
  • Sends written notes after each session
  • Adjusts the plan based on your kid's actual needs
  • Encourages rest days and full-body recovery

What you're actually paying for

$80–$120 per session for 1:1 youth football training is standard in Utah. Lower than that often means inexperience. Higher than that should come with measurable outcomes — recruiting tape, college contacts, or recovery from a specific issue.

A solid 8-session package (typically $650–$900) is enough to move the needle on one specific skill. Don't pay for 16+ sessions until you've seen results from the first 4.
